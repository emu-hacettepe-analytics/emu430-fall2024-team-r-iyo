Key takeaways

  • The dataset records 215 cargo movements across 17 port authorities, 69 facilities, and 180 distinct companies, totaling ~109 million metric tons of hazardous cargo in 2022.
  • Hazardous cargo concentration is geographically lopsided, İzmir (Aliağa), Adana (Ceyhan), and Hatay (İskenderun) absorb the bulk; most provinces see only fractional volumes.
  • Company-level tonnage is steeply right-skewed: a heavy upper tail extends past 10⁷ tons while the bulk of receivers cluster in the 10³–10⁴ range.
  • About six in ten companies (107 / 180) cross the 20,000-ton threshold, but those companies carry ~99.5% of all hazardous tonnage. The policy net is wide by headcount, extremely concentrated by liability.
  • A hypothetical ₺50/ton levy on threshold-exceeding companies would yield approximately ₺5.4 billion in revenue, with the top-ten handlers contributing the majority of the bill.

4. The 20,000-ton question, a hypothetical tax simulation

The Directorate is considering tax/regulatory measures targeting companies that exceed 20,000 tons of hazardous cargo per year. To put a number on what that would mean, we apply a hypothetical ₺50 per ton levy on the full tonnage of any company that crosses the threshold.

Disclaimer. ₺50/ton is illustrative, chosen for arithmetic clarity, not based on any draft policy. Treat absolute figures as proportions, not forecasts.

Show code 
TAX_RATE_TL_PER_TON <- 50

co_tax <- company_totals |>
  mutate(
    above_threshold = total_mt > 20000,
    tax_TL = if_else(above_threshold, total_mt * TAX_RATE_TL_PER_TON, 0)
  )

threshold_summary <- co_tax |>
  summarise(
    n_total          = n(),
    n_taxed          = sum(above_threshold),
    pct_companies    = mean(above_threshold) * 100,
    total_mt         = sum(total_mt),
    taxed_mt         = sum(total_mt[above_threshold]),
    pct_taxed_mt     = sum(total_mt[above_threshold]) / sum(total_mt) * 100,
    total_tax_TL     = sum(tax_TL),
    total_tax_billion= sum(tax_TL) / 1e9
  )

Sensitivity to the threshold

What if the threshold weren’t 20,000 tons? The plot below traces how the number of taxpayers and the share of tonnage taxed move as we slide it.

Show code 
thresholds <- c(5e3, 1e4, 2e4, 5e4, 1e5, 2.5e5, 5e5, 1e6)

sens <- tibble(threshold = thresholds) |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(
    n_taxed     = sum(company_totals$total_mt > threshold),
    share_mt    = sum(company_totals$total_mt[company_totals$total_mt > threshold]) /
                  sum(company_totals$total_mt) * 100
  ) |>
  ungroup()

ggplot(sens, aes(x = threshold)) +
  geom_line(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_point(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", size = 3) +
  geom_text(
    aes(y = share_mt, label = sprintf("n=%d", n_taxed)),
    vjust = -1, color = "#00838F", fontface = "bold", size = 3.4, family = "Inter"
  ) +
  scale_x_log10(labels = scales::label_number(big.mark = ",", scale_cut = scales::cut_short_scale())) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 105), labels = function(x) paste0(x, "%")) +
  labs(
    title    = "How sharp is the cliff at 20,000 tons?",
    subtitle = "Even a 5x or 10x stricter threshold barely loses tonnage coverage, the giants dominate.",
    x = "Threshold (metric tons, log scale)",
    y = "Share of total tonnage above threshold",
    caption = "Annotations show the number of companies caught at each threshold."
  ) +
  theme_riyo()

The take-away: because the upper tail is so heavy, moving the threshold up by an order of magnitude barely reduces tonnage coverage, but it sharply reduces the number of companies subject to the policy. That’s a useful lever for designers who want a high-impact, low-friction policy.

6. By coastal region: where does the spread sit?

Aggregating to province level can let outliers (a single mega-refinery, a single LPG terminal) dominate the picture. Grouping ports by Türkiye’s four coastal regions gives a more balanced view of how typical hazardous cargo movements compare across the country, alongside the giants. The box plot below shows every leaf record in the dataset, plotted on a logarithmic axis to keep both small and very large shipments visible. The box itself shows the middle 50% of cargo movements, the line inside the box is the median, and the dots above the whiskers are outliers, often individual mega-shipments.

The four regions tell different stories. Marmara and the Black Sea share the highest typical (median) cargo size per record, both sitting around the 50,000-ton mark, reflecting the steady volume of LPG and petroleum terminals around İstanbul, Tekirdağ, Samsun and Karadeniz Ereğli. The Mediterranean has the lowest median yet hosts the most extreme outliers, driven by a handful of mega-shipments at Ceyhan and İskenderun that sit far above the rest of the region. The Aegean’s median falls in the middle, but its outliers (the Aliağa refinery and Star Aliağa terminal) are among the largest single records in the entire dataset. Across all four regions, the gap between an average shipment and the regional giant spans three to four orders of magnitude, which is the recurring shape of this dataset: a steady base of routine handling on top of a thin layer of exceptional volume.

7. Conclusions

  • Geography: Türkiye’s hazardous cargo handling is not evenly distributed. A small set of provinces, driven by refining and large export terminals, dominates national volume.
  • Industry structure: The market is a classic heavy-tailed distribution. Many small handlers, a few giants moving most of the tonnage.
  • Policy implication: Because volume is so concentrated, a tonnage-based levy is administratively narrow, it would touch a minority of companies but capture most of the activity. That makes it both easy to enforce and politically charged.
  • Sensitivity: Moving the threshold from 20,000 to 100,000 or 200,000 tons would not meaningfully reduce coverage of total tonnage but would significantly reduce the number of regulated firms, a real lever for policy designers.

## 4. The 20,000-ton question, a hypothetical tax simulation

The Directorate is considering tax/regulatory measures targeting companies that exceed **20,000 tons** of hazardous cargo per year. To put a number on what that would mean, we apply a hypothetical **₺50 per ton** levy on the *full* tonnage of any company that crosses the threshold.

> **Disclaimer.** ₺50/ton is illustrative, chosen for arithmetic clarity, not based on any draft policy. Treat absolute figures as proportions, not forecasts.

### Sensitivity to the threshold

What if the threshold weren't 20,000 tons? The plot below traces how the **number of taxpayers** and the **share of tonnage taxed** move as we slide it.

```{r}
#| label: threshold-sensitivity
#| fig-width: 9
#| fig-height: 4.5
#| fig-cap: "Sensitivity of the policy to the threshold value."
thresholds <- c(5e3, 1e4, 2e4, 5e4, 1e5, 2.5e5, 5e5, 1e6)

sens <- tibble(threshold = thresholds) |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(
    n_taxed     = sum(company_totals$total_mt > threshold),
    share_mt    = sum(company_totals$total_mt[company_totals$total_mt > threshold]) /
                  sum(company_totals$total_mt) * 100
  ) |>
  ungroup()

ggplot(sens, aes(x = threshold)) +
  geom_line(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_point(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", size = 3) +
  geom_text(
    aes(y = share_mt, label = sprintf("n=%d", n_taxed)),
    vjust = -1, color = "#00838F", fontface = "bold", size = 3.4, family = "Inter"
  ) +
  scale_x_log10(labels = scales::label_number(big.mark = ",", scale_cut = scales::cut_short_scale())) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 105), labels = function(x) paste0(x, "%")) +
  labs(
    title    = "How sharp is the cliff at 20,000 tons?",
    subtitle = "Even a 5x or 10x stricter threshold barely loses tonnage coverage, the giants dominate.",
```

The take-away: because the upper tail is so heavy, **moving the threshold up by an order of magnitude barely reduces tonnage coverage**, but it sharply reduces the *number* of companies subject to the policy. That's a useful lever for designers who want a high-impact, low-friction policy.

## 6. By coastal region: where does the spread sit?

Grouping ports by Türkiye's four coastal regions gives a more balanced view of how *typical* hazardous cargo movements compare across the country, alongside the giants. The box plot below shows every leaf record in the dataset, plotted on a logarithmic axis to keep both small and very large shipments visible. The box itself shows the middle 50% of cargo movements, the line inside the box is the median, and the dots above the whiskers are outliers, often individual mega-shipments.

## 7. Conclusions

-   **Geography:** Türkiye's hazardous cargo handling is **not evenly distributed**. A small set of provinces, driven by refining and large export terminals, dominates national volume.
-   **Industry structure:** The market is a **classic heavy-tailed distribution**. Many small handlers, a few giants moving most of the tonnage.
-   **Policy implication:** Because volume is so concentrated, a tonnage-based levy is **administratively narrow**, it would touch a minority of companies but capture most of the activity. That makes it both **easy to enforce** and **politically charged**.
-   **Sensitivity:** Moving the threshold from 20,000 to 100,000 or 200,000 tons would *not* meaningfully reduce coverage of total tonnage but would *significantly* reduce the number of regulated firms, a real lever for policy designers.
