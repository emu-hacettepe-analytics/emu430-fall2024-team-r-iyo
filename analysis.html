Key Takeaways

  • The dataset includes 215 cargo movements from 17 port authorities, 69 facilities and 180 different companies with approximately 109 million metric tons of hazardous cargo reported in 2022. Geographic concentration is striking İzmir (Aliağa), Adana (Ceyhan) and Hatay (İskenderun) dominate the volume while most other provinces handle only a sliver of the total.

  • Company-level tonnage is deeply uneven. Just 107 of the 180 companies clear the 20,000 ton threshold but together they move roughly 99.5% of all hazardous cargo. Wide by headcount, narrow by liability.

  • A hypothetical ₺50/ton levy on those threshold crossing companies would bring in around ₺5.4 billion most of it coming from the top ten handlers alone.

The dataset is a long table where each row pairs a receiver company with the facility that handled their cargo and the regional port authority that oversees that facility. The numeric column cargo_mt is what we’ll be slicing in every direction below.

4. Simulated Tax Impact Based on Hazardous Cargo Handling Amounts

The Directorate is weighing tax and regulatory measures for companies that move more than 20,000 tons of hazardous cargo in a given year. To get a sense of the scale, we run a simple simulation: a hypothetical ₺50 per ton levy applied to the full tonnage of any company that crosses that line.

Note. The ₺50/ton figure is illustrative picked for ease of calculation, not drawn from any draft policy. The totals below are best read as proportions, not forecasts.

Show code 
TAX_RATE_TL_PER_TON <- 50

co_tax <- company_totals |>
  mutate(
    above_threshold = total_mt > 20000,
    tax_TL = if_else(above_threshold, total_mt * TAX_RATE_TL_PER_TON, 0)
  )

threshold_summary <- co_tax |>
  summarise(
    n_total          = n(),
    n_taxed          = sum(above_threshold),
    pct_companies    = mean(above_threshold) * 100,
    taxed_mt         = sum(total_mt[above_threshold]),
    total_mt         = sum(total_mt),
    pct_taxed_mt     = taxed_mt / total_mt * 100,
    total_tax_TL     = sum(tax_TL),
    total_tax_billion= sum(tax_TL) / 1e9
  )

Sensitivity to the threshold

What if the threshold weren’t 20,000 tons? The plot below traces how the number of taxpayers and the share of tonnage taxed move as we slide it.

Show code 
thresholds <- c(5e3, 1e4, 2e4, 5e4, 1e5, 2.5e5, 5e5, 1e6)

sens <- tibble(threshold = thresholds) |>
  rowwise() |>
  mutate(
    n_taxed     = sum(company_totals$total_mt > threshold),
    share_mt    = sum(company_totals$total_mt[company_totals$total_mt > threshold]) /
                  sum(company_totals$total_mt) * 100
  ) |>
  ungroup()

ggplot(sens, aes(x = threshold)) +
  geom_line(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", linewidth = 1.2) +
  geom_point(aes(y = share_mt), color = "#C2185B", size = 3) +
  geom_text(
    aes(y = share_mt, label = sprintf("n=%d", n_taxed)),
    vjust = -1, color = "#00838F", fontface = "bold", size = 3.4, family = "Inter"
  ) +
  scale_x_log10(labels = scales::label_number(big.mark = ",", scale_cut = scales::cut_short_scale())) +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 105), labels = function(x) paste0(x, "%")) +
  labs(
    title    = "How sharp is the cliff at 20,000 tons?",
    subtitle = "Even a 5x or 10x stricter threshold barely loses tonnage coverage, the giants dominate.",
    x = "Threshold (metric tons, log scale)",
    y = "Share of total tonnage above threshold",
    caption = "Annotations show the number of companies caught at each threshold."
  ) +
  theme_riyo()

Because the upper tail of the distribution is very large, increasing the threshold significantly reduces the number of companies affected by the policy, while the total amount of covered cargo decreases only slightly. This may help policymakers design a regulation that targets major cargo handlers without affecting too many companies.

6. By coastal region: where does the spread sit?

Aggregating to province level can let outliers like a single megarefinery or a single LPG terminal dominate the picture. Grouping ports by Türkiye’s four coastal regions gives a more balanced view of how typical hazardous cargo movements compare across the country, alongside the giants. The box plot below shows every record in the dataset on a logarithmic axis, keeping both small and very large shipments visible. The box itself covers the middle 50% of cargo movements, the line inside is the median and the dots beyond the whiskers are outliers, often individual megashipments.

The four regions tell different stories. Marmara and the Black Sea share the highest typical (median) cargo size per record, both sitting around the 50,000 ton mark, reflecting the steady volume of LPG and petroleum terminals around İstanbul, Tekirdağ, Samsun and Karadeniz Ereğli. The Mediterranean has the lowest median yet hosts the most extreme outliers, driven by a handful of megashipments at Ceyhan and İskenderun that sit far above the rest of the region. The Aegean’s median falls in the middle but its outliers (the Aliağa refinery and Star Aliağa terminal) are among the largest single records in the entire dataset. Across all four regions, the gap between an average shipment and the regional giant spans three to four orders of magnitude which is the recurring shape of this dataset: a steady base of routine handling on top of a thin layer of exceptional volume.

7. Conclusions

  • Geography: Türkiye’s hazardous cargo handling is not evenly distributed. A small set of provinces driven by refining and large export terminals, dominates national volume.
  • Industry structure: The market is a classic heavy tailed distribution. Many small handlers, a few giants moving most of the tonnage.
  • Policy implication: Because volume is so concentrated, a tonnagebased levy is administratively narrow. It would touch a minority of companies but capture most of the activity. That makes it both easy to enforce and politically charged.
  • Sensitivity: Moving the threshold from 20,000 to 100,000 or 200,000 tons would not meaningfully reduce coverage of total tonnage but would significantly reduce the number of regulated firms a real lever for policy designers.

## 4. Simulated Tax Impact Based on Hazardous Cargo Handling Amounts

The Directorate is weighing tax and regulatory measures for companies that move more than **20,000 tons** of hazardous cargo in a given year. To get a sense of the scale, we run a simple simulation: a hypothetical **₺50 per ton** levy applied to the full tonnage of any company that crosses that line.

> **Note.** The ₺50/ton figure is illustrative picked for ease of calculation, not drawn from any draft policy. The totals below are best read as proportions, not forecasts.

```{r}
#| label: tax-simulation
TAX_RATE_TL_PER_TON <- 50
```

### Sensitivity to the threshold

What if the threshold weren't 20,000 tons? The plot below traces how the **number of taxpayers** and the **share of tonnage taxed** move as we slide it.

```{r}
#| label: threshold-sensitivity

#| fig-height: 4.5

thresholds <- c(5e3, 1e4, 2e4, 5e4, 1e5, 2.5e5, 5e5, 1e6)

sens <- tibble(threshold = thresholds) |>

  ) +
  scale_x_log10(labels = scales::label_number(big.mark = ",", scale_cut = scales::cut_short_scale())) +
```

Because the upper tail of the distribution is very large, increasing the threshold significantly reduces the number of companies affected by the policy, while the total amount of covered cargo decreases only slightly. This may help policymakers design a regulation that targets major cargo handlers without affecting too many companies.

## 6. By coastal region: where does the spread sit?

Aggregating to province level can let outliers like a single megarefinery or a single LPG terminal dominate the picture. Grouping ports by Türkiye's four coastal regions gives a more balanced view of how typical hazardous cargo movements compare across the country, alongside the giants. The box plot below shows every record in the dataset on a logarithmic axis, keeping both small and very large shipments visible. The box itself covers the middle 50% of cargo movements, the line inside is the median and the dots beyond the whiskers are outliers, often individual megashipments.

The four regions tell different stories. Marmara and the Black Sea share the highest *typical* (median) cargo size per record, both sitting around the 50,000 ton mark, reflecting the steady volume of LPG and petroleum terminals around İstanbul, Tekirdağ, Samsun and Karadeniz Ereğli. Across all four regions, the gap between an average shipment and the regional giant spans three to four orders of magnitude which is the recurring shape of this dataset: **a steady base of routine handling on top of a thin layer of exceptional volume.**

## 7. Conclusions

-   **Geography:** Türkiye's hazardous cargo handling is **not evenly distributed**. A small set of provinces driven by refining and large export terminals, dominates national volume.
-   **Industry structure:** The market is a **classic heavy tailed distribution**. Many small handlers, a few giants moving most of the tonnage.
-   **Policy implication:** Because volume is so concentrated, a tonnagebased levy is **administratively narrow.** It would touch a minority of companies but capture most of the activity. That makes it both **easy to enforce** and **politically charged**.
-   **Sensitivity:** Moving the threshold from 20,000 to 100,000 or 200,000 tons would *not* meaningfully reduce coverage of total tonnage but would *significantly* reduce the number of regulated firms a real lever for policy designers.
